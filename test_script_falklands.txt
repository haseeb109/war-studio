TITLE: 8,000 Miles From Home: How Britain Fought the Falklands War

On the second of April 1982, a small group of Royal Marines stood in the dark outside Government House in Stanley, the tiny capital of the Falkland Islands. There were fewer than seventy of them. Coming ashore around them were hundreds of Argentine special forces and marines, backed by armored amphibious vehicles. By the end of the morning, the British governor had surrendered, and the Argentine flag was flying over a group of islands most people in Britain could not have found on a map.

Eight thousand miles away in London, the government faced a question no modern military had ever really answered. Could a country send a full fleet to the other end of the planet, land an army on hostile islands in the middle of the South Atlantic winter, and win, without a single friendly airfield anywhere nearby?

This is the story of how they tried.

To understand why this war was so hard, you have to start with the map. The Falkland Islands sit in the South Atlantic Ocean, about 300 miles east of the coast of Argentina. There are two main islands, East Falkland and West Falkland, separated by a narrow channel called Falkland Sound, plus hundreds of smaller islands. In 1982, around 1,800 people lived there, most of them sheep farmers of British descent.

Argentina calls the islands Las Malvinas and had claimed them since the nineteenth century. Britain had held them since 1833. For decades, the two countries argued over sovereignty through diplomats. Then, in 1982, the military government in Buenos Aires decided to stop talking.

The junta, led by General Leopoldo Galtieri, was in serious trouble at home. Inflation was running out of control, the economy was collapsing, and protests were growing in the streets. Taking back the Malvinas would be enormously popular. And the generals believed Britain would never fight for a few windswept islands so far away.

They had reasons to think so. Britain had been shrinking its navy for years. The only Royal Navy ship stationed in the region, the ice patrol vessel HMS Endurance, was due to be scrapped. Plans were already in place to sell one of Britain's two small aircraft carriers to Australia.

The invasion, called Operation Rosario, went almost perfectly. Argentine forces landed near Stanley before dawn. The Royal Marines of Naval Party 8901 fought for about three hours, damaged one Argentine armored vehicle, and then received the order to lay down their arms. A day later, Argentine forces also seized South Georgia, a remote island about 800 miles further east.

In London, Prime Minister Margaret Thatcher called her cabinet together. The First Sea Lord, Admiral Sir Henry Leach, arrived in full uniform and told her that the navy could put together a task force within days, and that it could retake the islands. It was a bold promise. Many senior officials privately doubted it.

Within seventy-two hours, the first ships were leaving port.

The task force that sailed from Portsmouth and Plymouth was assembled at astonishing speed. At its heart were two aircraft carriers, HMS Hermes and HMS Invincible. Neither was a big American-style supercarrier. Together they carried just twenty Sea Harrier jump jets, small subsonic fighters that could take off from a short deck and land vertically.

Around the carriers sailed destroyers and frigates, including the new Type 42 destroyers armed with Sea Dart missiles and the Type 22 frigates carrying the short-range Sea Wolf system. Behind them came amphibious assault ships, supply vessels, and dozens of civilian ships taken up from trade.

That last part is often forgotten. Britain requisitioned more than forty merchant ships. The luxury liner Canberra became a troop transport. The Queen Elizabeth 2 carried an entire brigade south. Container ships were converted into floating aircraft hangars, and cross-channel ferries were turned into supply carriers.

The problem was distance. From Britain to the Falklands is roughly 8,000 miles, about the same as flying from London to Sydney. The only place to stop on the way was Ascension Island, a speck of British territory in the middle of the Atlantic, close to the equator and about 3,900 miles from the Falklands.

Ascension became the most important staging post of the war. Its single runway at Wideawake Airfield went from handling a few flights a week to becoming one of the busiest airports in the world. Supplies, weapons and troops were flown in from Britain, sorted, and loaded onto the ships heading south.

Even from Ascension, the islands were still far beyond the range of any British combat aircraft. To show that it could strike the Falklands from the air, the Royal Air Force planned one of the longest bombing missions in history.

It was called Operation Black Buck.

On the night of the thirtieth of April, a single Avro Vulcan bomber took off from Ascension carrying twenty-one 1,000-pound bombs. Its target was the runway at Stanley airport, almost 4,000 miles away. The Vulcan could not fly that far on its own fuel. To get it there, the RAF used a fleet of Victor tanker aircraft that refueled the bomber, and each other, again and again over the dark ocean.

In total, eleven Victor tankers were needed to get one bomber to its target and back. The round trip took nearly sixteen hours and covered close to 8,000 miles. Just before dawn on the first of May, the Vulcan crossed the coast of East Falkland and released its bombs across the runway at a shallow angle.

One bomb hit the middle of the runway and left a large crater. The damage was repaired, and Argentine transport planes kept using the strip until the end of the war. But the raid sent a message. If British bombers could reach Stanley, they could in theory reach the Argentine mainland too. Argentina responded by keeping many of its best fighter jets at home to defend its own cities.

Meanwhile, the war at sea was about to turn deadly.

By the end of April, the British had declared a Total Exclusion Zone, a circle 200 nautical miles wide around the Falklands. Any Argentine ship or aircraft inside it could be attacked. Outside it, an Argentine naval group was moving.

The cruiser ARA General Belgrano was an old warship. It had been built in the United States before the Second World War, and had survived the attack on Pearl Harbor in 1941 under the name USS Phoenix. Argentina bought it in 1951. In 1982 it carried fifteen six-inch guns and more than a thousand sailors, and it was escorted by two destroyers armed with Exocet anti-ship missiles.

The British nuclear submarine HMS Conqueror had been shadowing the Belgrano for more than a day, south of the Falklands near the Burdwood Bank. The cruiser was outside the exclusion zone, sailing on a course that took it away from the task force. But British commanders feared it was part of a pincer movement, with the Argentine aircraft carrier Veinticinco de Mayo approaching from the north.

On the second of May, the war cabinet changed the rules of engagement. That afternoon, Conqueror fired three Mark 8 torpedoes, an old design dating back to the 1920s. Two of them struck the Belgrano. The first blew a hole near the bow. The second hit near the stern, tearing through the engine room and the crew areas.

Within twenty minutes, the captain gave the order to abandon ship. The Belgrano sank in the freezing South Atlantic. 323 Argentine sailors died, almost half of all the Argentine lives lost in the entire war.

The sinking is still argued about today. To some, it was a legal act of war against a warship that posed a threat. To others, it was a deliberate escalation that killed hundreds of men on a ship sailing away from the fighting. Whatever the view, the effect was immediate. The Argentine navy pulled its surface ships, including its aircraft carrier, back to its home ports. For the rest of the war, they never seriously threatened the British fleet again.

But the Argentine air force and naval aviation were about to show what they could do.

Two days later, on the fourth of May, two Argentine navy Super Etendard jets took off from the Rio Grande base in Tierra del Fuego, at the southern tip of Argentina. Each carried a single AM39 Exocet, a French-built sea-skimming missile that flies just a few feet above the waves.

The Super Etendards flew low over the sea toward the British fleet, refueled from a tanker on the way, then climbed briefly to switch on their radars. They found a target about twenty miles away and fired. One missile slammed into the destroyer HMS Sheffield, one of the Type 42s that was supposed to protect the carriers from exactly this kind of attack.

The missile's warhead may not even have exploded. It did not need to. Burning rocket fuel set the ship on fire, and the fire spread quickly through the hull. Twenty men died. After days of trying to save her, Sheffield was taken under tow and sank on the tenth of May. It was the first British warship lost to enemy action since the Second World War, and the shock in Britain was enormous.

Argentina had only five Exocet missiles of this type at the start of the war. France had halted further deliveries. Every one of the remaining missiles would be hunted, feared, and talked about on both sides.

Back in London, the generals had chosen the place to land. Not near Stanley, where Argentine defenses were strongest, but on the opposite side of East Falkland, at a sheltered inlet called San Carlos Water, off Falkland Sound, about 50 miles west of the capital.

San Carlos was a clever choice. Hills surrounded the inlet, making it hard for low-flying jets to line up an attack. It was far from the main Argentine garrison. And the Argentines did not expect it.

Before dawn on the twenty-first of May, the landings began. Landing craft carried Royal Marines from 3 Commando Brigade and soldiers of the Parachute Regiment onto several beaches around the inlet. By the end of the day, around 4,000 British troops were ashore, with almost no resistance on the ground.

In the air, it was a different story.

Argentine pilots flew from mainland bases at Rio Grande, Rio Gallegos, San Julian and Comodoro Rivadavia, more than 400 miles away. Their A-4 Skyhawks and Israeli-built Daggers had only a few minutes of fuel over the islands. They came in at extremely low altitude, sometimes just fifty feet above the water, weaving through the hills and racing down Falkland Sound toward the ships.

The British sailors called the narrow channel Bomb Alley.

Over five days, the attacks hardly stopped. On the twenty-first of May, the frigate HMS Ardent was hit by bombs again and again and sank in Grantham Sound. On the twenty-third, HMS Antelope was struck by a bomb that did not explode. That evening, while a specialist tried to defuse it, the bomb went off. The ship burned through the night, broke apart, and sank, a moment captured in one of the most famous photographs of the war.

On the twenty-fifth of May, Argentina's national day, the destroyer HMS Coventry was hit by three bombs north of Pebble Island and capsized in about twenty minutes. Nineteen men died.

That same afternoon, the Super Etendards came back. Two more Exocets were fired at the fleet. Chaff from the warships drew one of them away, and it locked on to the next large target it could find. That was the container ship Atlantic Conveyor.

Atlantic Conveyor was a merchant vessel carrying vital equipment for the land campaign, including Chinook and Wessex helicopters, tents for thousands of men, and the material to build a temporary runway. The Exocet hit her side and started a fire that could not be stopped. Twelve men died, including her captain. Only one Chinook helicopter survived, because it happened to be in the air at the time.

The loss of those helicopters changed the land war. The plan had been to fly troops and guns across East Falkland toward Stanley. Now, most of them would have to walk.

Yet here is something that is often forgotten. Many of the Argentine bombs that hit British ships did not explode. They were released so low that their fuses did not have time to arm before they struck. Military historians believe that if those bombs had been fused correctly, Britain could have lost several more ships, and possibly the war.

And the Argentine air force paid a terrible price. British Sea Harriers armed with the new AIM-9L Sidewinder missile shot down around twenty Argentine aircraft in air combat, without losing a single Harrier in a dogfight. Ship-launched missiles and ground fire from San Carlos destroyed many more. By the end of the war, Argentina had lost around 100 aircraft of all types.

With the beachhead secure, the British started moving inland.

The first target was a small settlement south of San Carlos, at a narrow neck of land called Goose Green, with an airstrip used by Argentine Pucara ground-attack aircraft. On the night of the twenty-eighth of May, about 500 men of the 2nd Battalion, the Parachute Regiment, attacked a garrison of more than 1,000 Argentine troops dug into trenches on open ground.

The battle lasted around fourteen hours. The paratroopers had little artillery support and almost no cover, and they were short on ammunition by the afternoon. Their commanding officer, Lieutenant Colonel Herbert Jones, known as H, was killed leading a charge against an Argentine machine-gun position on a hill near Darwin. He was later awarded the Victoria Cross, Britain's highest award for bravery.

The next morning, the Argentine commander at Goose Green surrendered. Around 1,000 prisoners were taken, far more than the attacking force itself. The British lost seventeen men. Goose Green proved to both sides that the British infantry could beat a larger force dug into prepared positions.

While 2 Para fought at Goose Green, the rest of the force began its march east. Because the helicopters had gone down with Atlantic Conveyor, Royal Marines of 45 Commando and soldiers of 3 Para set out on foot across the island, carrying packs weighing over 100 pounds through bog, rock and freezing rain.

The Marines called it yomping. The paratroopers called it tabbing. Whatever the name, they covered around 50 miles in a few days, from San Carlos through Douglas and Teal Inlet, until they were in the hills overlooking Stanley.

To speed things up, a second brigade, the 5th Infantry Brigade, including the Welsh Guards and Scots Guards, was sent around the south of the island by sea. On the eighth of June, two landing ships, Sir Galahad and Sir Tristram, were waiting in daylight at a cove called Port Pleasant near Fitzroy, south of Bluff Cove, with soldiers still aboard.

Argentine Skyhawks found them. Bombs hit both ships, and Sir Galahad turned into an inferno. Forty-eight men were killed, most of them Welsh Guards, and many more were badly burned. It was the single worst day of the war for British forces.

By the second week of June, the British had surrounded Stanley on three sides. Between them and the town was a ring of rocky, steep hills, each one held by dug-in Argentine troops with machine guns, mortars and artillery. The names of those hills would become famous in British military history: Mount Longdon, Two Sisters, Mount Harriet, Wireless Ridge, Tumbledown and Mount William.

The British commander, Major General Jeremy Moore, decided to attack at night. The British believed their soldiers were better trained for close fighting in the dark, and that darkness would protect them from Argentine guns as they crossed the open ground.

On the night of the eleventh of June, three attacks went in at once. 3 Para attacked Mount Longdon in the north. Two Sisters was taken by 45 Commando, while 42 Commando hit Mount Harriet from the south after a long march around its side.

The fighting on Mount Longdon was the hardest. It lasted around twelve hours, often hand to hand, among the rocks, with bayonets and grenades. The Argentine conscripts and marines there fought hard. By the morning, 3 Para had taken the mountain, but twenty-three of its soldiers were dead. A sergeant named Ian McKay, killed while attacking a machine-gun position, was awarded the second Victoria Cross of the war.

Out at sea that same night, the last Argentine Exocet attack of the war took place. Argentine technicians had taken missiles from a destroyer and mounted them on a trailer near Stanley, firing from land. One missile hit the destroyer HMS Glamorgan off the coast, killing fourteen men. The ship survived, but it showed that the threat was never completely gone.

Two nights later, on the thirteenth of June, the final assaults began. The Scots Guards attacked Mount Tumbledown, the highest point before the town. The Argentine 5th Marine Infantry Battalion, one of Argentina's best units, held the summit. The battle went on through the night in the snow and wind.

At the same time, 2 Para, the same battalion that had fought at Goose Green, attacked Wireless Ridge to the north, supported by light tanks of the Blues and Royals, artillery, and the guns of Royal Navy ships firing from the sea. By dawn on the fourteenth of June, both positions had fallen.

From the top of the hills, the British soldiers could see Stanley below them. Argentine troops were streaming back into the town. The defense was collapsing.

That afternoon, General Mario Menendez, the Argentine commander in the Falklands, agreed to talk. That evening, he signed the surrender. Around 9,800 Argentine troops laid down their arms, and the British flag was raised again over Government House, seventy-four days after it had come down.

The cost had been heavy on both sides. 255 British servicemen were killed, along with three Falkland Islanders who died during the shelling of Stanley. Argentina lost 649 men, almost half of them on the General Belgrano. Britain lost two destroyers, two frigates, a landing ship and the Atlantic Conveyor, along with dozens of aircraft and helicopters.

In Buenos Aires, the defeat was the end for the military junta. Within days, General Galtieri was forced out. A year later, Argentina held free elections and returned to civilian rule, after seven years of military government. In Britain, the victory transformed Margaret Thatcher's position, and she won a landslide election the following year.

But the military lessons of the war went far beyond politics.

The first lesson was the power of a single missile. A few Exocets, launched by a handful of jets, sank or crippled ships that had cost tens of millions of pounds. Navies around the world rushed to add close-in weapons that could shoot down missiles in the last seconds of their flight, systems like the American Phalanx and the Dutch Goalkeeper.

The second lesson was about fire. British warships of that era used aluminum in their upper structures and materials that burned and gave off toxic smoke. After Sheffield, navies redesigned ships to resist fire, and changed the clothing sailors wore at sea.

The third lesson was about airborne radar. The British fleet had no early warning aircraft that could spot low-flying jets from far away. That is why the Skyhawks could appear over the hills with almost no warning. Within months of the war, Britain fitted radar to Sea King helicopters to fill the gap, and every modern carrier group now flies some form of airborne early warning.

The fourth lesson was logistics. The war was won as much by tankers, merchant ships and supply chains as by soldiers and pilots. Without Ascension Island, without the requisitioned liners and container ships, and without aircraft refueling each other across thousands of miles, the task force could never have reached the islands, let alone fought there.

And the final lesson was about people. Argentina had brave pilots and some excellent units, but much of its army in the Falklands was made up of young conscripts, poorly fed, poorly equipped, and left to freeze in their trenches. The British force was smaller, but every unit was made of professional volunteers trained for exactly this kind of war.

Today, the Falkland Islands are still British. A permanent garrison is based at RAF Mount Pleasant, a large airfield built after the war about 30 miles southwest of Stanley, where Typhoon fighter jets stand ready. Argentina still claims the islands, and the issue still matters deeply in both countries.

On the hills above Stanley, the old Argentine trenches are still there, filled with peat and rain. Every year, veterans from both sides return to Mount Longdon, Tumbledown and Goose Green, to remember a short, brutal war fought at the bottom of the world.

Seventy-four days. Eight thousand miles. And a war that showed the world that even in the age of missiles and jets, distance, weather and the will to fight can still decide everything.
