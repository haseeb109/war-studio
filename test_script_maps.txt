TITLE: Why the Strait of Hormuz Is the Most Dangerous Water on Earth

Twenty-one miles. That is all that separates Iran from Oman at the narrowest point of the Strait of Hormuz. And through that gap, every single day, passes roughly one fifth of all the oil the world burns.

If this strait closes, even for a week, fuel prices spike from Tokyo to Texas. Factories slow down. Airlines ground flights. And that is exactly why the United States Navy has kept warships in these waters for more than forty years.

To understand why this place is so dangerous, you have to look at the map.

The Persian Gulf is a long, shallow body of water, about six hundred miles from end to end. On its northern shore sits Iran. On its southern shore, Saudi Arabia, Kuwait, Bahrain, Qatar and the United Arab Emirates. Almost all of them pump oil, and almost all of that oil has to leave the Gulf by ship.

There is only one way out. South past Qatar, east past Dubai, and then through the Strait of Hormuz into the Gulf of Oman and the Arabian Sea.

Inside the strait, the shipping lanes are even narrower than the strait itself. Two channels, each just two miles wide, one for ships going in and one for ships coming out, with a two mile buffer between them. A supertanker the length of three football fields has to thread that needle while Iranian islands watch from both sides.

And those islands matter. Look at the western entrance of the strait. Abu Musa, Greater Tunb and Lesser Tunb sit right next to the inbound and outbound lanes. Iran seized them in 1971, and the United Arab Emirates still claims them today. Iran has turned them into fortified outposts, with radar, anti-ship missiles and fast attack boats stationed just a few miles from passing tankers.

Further east, Qeshm Island stretches along the Iranian coast like a wall. It is the largest island in the Gulf, and much of it is honeycombed with tunnels. Iranian state television has shown underground missile cities there, long corridors lined with launchers ready to roll out, fire and disappear again.

Across the water on the mainland sits Bandar Abbas. This is the headquarters of Iran's navy and the main base of the Revolutionary Guard naval forces. From here, Iran can watch every ship that enters or leaves the Gulf.

So what would Iran actually use if it decided to close the strait?

First, mines. Iran is believed to have thousands of naval mines, from old contact mines to modern rocket-propelled designs that sit on the seabed and rise toward a passing hull. Because the strait is so shallow, mines are easy to lay and hard to find. In 1988, a single Iranian mine nearly sank the American frigate USS Samuel B. Roberts in the central Gulf, east of Bahrain.

Second, missiles. Along the Iranian coast, from Bushehr in the north down to Jask near the Gulf of Oman, Iran has positioned mobile anti-ship missile batteries. Some of these missiles can reach anywhere in the strait within minutes. From the coast near Bandar Abbas, the entire shipping channel sits inside their range.

Third, swarms. The Revolutionary Guard operates hundreds of small, fast boats armed with machine guns, rockets and torpedoes. Their plan is simple. Dozens of boats rush out from hidden coves along the coast and from the islands at the same time, coming from several directions, hoping to overwhelm a warship's defenses before it can shoot them all.

Fourth, drones and submarines. Iran flies drones over the strait every week, and it operates small Ghadir class midget submarines built for exactly these shallow waters, along with larger Kilo class submarines based at Bandar Abbas.

Now look at the other side of the map.

Across the Gulf, in Bahrain, sits the headquarters of the United States Fifth Fleet. From the port of Manama, American destroyers, minesweepers and patrol ships cover the whole Gulf. South of Bahrain, in Qatar, is Al Udeid Air Base, the largest American air base in the Middle East, home to tankers, bombers and command aircraft.

In the United Arab Emirates, near Abu Dhabi, Al Dhafra Air Base hosts American fighter jets, surveillance planes and drones, just a short flight from the strait itself. And outside the strait, in the Arabian Sea, an American aircraft carrier strike group often sails, far enough to stay out of the narrow water but close enough to launch jets within the hour.

This is the real standoff. Iran holds the northern shore and the islands. The United States and its partners hold the southern shore and the open sea beyond. Between them runs a channel just a few miles wide, crowded with tankers.

It has come to shooting before.

In 1987, during the Iran Iraq war, both sides were attacking tankers in the Gulf. The United States reflagged Kuwaiti tankers and escorted them north from the strait to Kuwait, past Iranian waters, in what became Operation Earnest Will. The convoys sailed more than five hundred miles up the Gulf under naval escort.

Then, in April 1988, after the Samuel B. Roberts hit that mine, the United States struck back in Operation Praying Mantis. American ships and aircraft destroyed two Iranian oil platforms used as military posts, sank an Iranian frigate and several fast boats, and badly damaged a second frigate near Qeshm Island. In a single day, Iran lost about half of its operational navy.

Tensions never really went away. In 2019, tankers were damaged by limpet mines off the port of Fujairah, just outside the strait on the Gulf of Oman. A month later, Iran shot down an American Global Hawk drone near the strait, claiming it had crossed into Iranian airspace near Kuh-e Mobarak. Then Revolutionary Guard boats seized the British tanker Stena Impero inside the strait and took it to Bandar Abbas.

Each time, the world held its breath, because everyone knows what a real closure would mean.

Here is how a conflict could unfold. Iranian boats lay mines in the inbound and outbound lanes at night. Missile batteries near Bandar Abbas and on Qeshm Island switch on their radars. Fast attack boats gather behind Abu Musa and the Tunbs. Tanker traffic stops almost immediately, because no ship owner will send a crew into a minefield.

The American response would come from every direction at once. Minesweepers from Bahrain move south to clear a path. Fighter jets from Al Dhafra and from the carrier in the Arabian Sea hunt the missile launchers along the Iranian coast. Destroyers push into the Gulf of Oman to protect the eastern entrance. Bombers from Al Udeid strike radar sites and naval bases from Bushehr to Jask.

Military planners believe the strait could be reopened. The hard part is how long it would take. Clearing mines in shallow, busy water is slow, careful work. Even a few weeks of closure could send oil prices to levels the world has never seen.

That is why the Gulf states have been building ways around the strait. Saudi Arabia runs the East West pipeline across the desert, from its oil fields near the Gulf coast all the way to the port of Yanbu on the Red Sea, more than seven hundred miles to the west. The United Arab Emirates built a pipeline from its oil fields near Habshan to Fujairah, on the Gulf of Oman, so its oil can leave without passing through Hormuz at all.

But those pipelines carry only a fraction of what the tankers move. Iraq, Kuwait, Qatar and Bahrain still depend almost entirely on the strait. Qatar alone ships a huge share of the world's liquefied natural gas from Ras Laffan, and every one of those gas carriers must sail through Hormuz.

And here is the twist that keeps the whole region in balance. Iran needs the strait too. Its own oil exports leave from Kharg Island, in the northern Gulf, west of Bushehr, and those tankers also have to pass through Hormuz to reach China. Closing the strait would cut off Iran's own income, and it would anger the biggest buyer of Iranian oil.

So the strait stays open, not because anyone trusts each other, but because closing it would hurt everyone.

There is also a second chokepoint that shows what could happen here. Far to the southwest, at the bottom of the Red Sea, lies the Bab-el-Mandeb strait, between Yemen and Djibouti. Since late 2023, Houthi forces in Yemen have fired missiles and drones at merchant ships passing through it, from launch sites near Hodeidah and along the Yemeni coast.

The result was dramatic. Many shipping companies stopped using the Red Sea and the Suez Canal altogether. Instead, their ships sailed all the way around Africa, past the Cape of Good Hope, adding roughly ten days and thousands of miles to every voyage between Asia and Europe.

American and British warships answered with Operation Prosperity Guardian, escorting ships and shooting down incoming missiles over the southern Red Sea. Jets launched from carriers in the Red Sea and the Gulf of Aden struck Houthi radar and launch sites inside Yemen. Yet even after months of strikes, the attacks did not fully stop.

Military analysts look at Bab-el-Mandeb and see a warning. The Houthis are a small force with limited weapons. Iran has a full navy, thousands of mines, and a coastline that runs the entire length of the Gulf. If a small group could disrupt one chokepoint for months, imagine what a country could do at the other.

Every day, warships from the United States, Britain, France and the Gulf states patrol these waters. Every day, Iranian boats shadow them, sometimes coming within a few hundred yards. Every day, around twenty million barrels of oil slip through a channel just two miles wide.

Twenty-one miles of water, between two coastlines that have been preparing for war with each other for more than four decades.

One mistake, one mine, one missile fired in panic, and the most important waterway on the planet could go dark overnight.
